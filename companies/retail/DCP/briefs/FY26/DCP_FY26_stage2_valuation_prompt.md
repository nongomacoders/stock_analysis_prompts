### 1. Executive Forensic Audit Summary

This forensic audit of Dis-Chem Pharmacies Limited's FY2026 integrated annual report and companion presentations reveals a group navigating a challenging consumer environment through significant strategic investment. The core retail business demonstrated underlying resilience, but this was masked by substantial, largely non-recurring expenditures related to the "X, bigly labs" ecosystem and the retirement of a legacy loyalty program.

**True Economic Performance:** The group's statutory revenue grew by a healthy 9.3% to R42.8bn. However, statutory operating profit and headline earnings per share (HEPS) declined by 11.9% and 17.3%, respectively. This decline is not a reflection of deteriorating core operations but is primarily attributable to a **R445 million investment in the ecosystem and non-recurring expenses**. Excluding these items, core retail profit before tax increased by a robust 27.1%, indicating strong underlying operational leverage. The key driver was a 12.2% growth in retail total income, which outpaced retail expense growth of 10.3% (excluding ecosystem investment).

**Balance Sheet Health & Liquidity:** The balance sheet shows a **tight but managed liquidity position**. The group reported a net cash deficit of R1.17bn, driven by a R1.67bn bank overdraft. This is a significant increase from the prior year's R732m deficit. While management asserts sufficient headroom on its R4.1bn working capital facilities and debt covenants, the reliance on short-term debt to fund operations and strategic investments is a key risk. The increase in the gearing ratio from 2.3x to 2.8x corroborates this rising leverage. The R500m additional bullet loan taken out post-year-end further underscores the need for external funding.

**Key Findings:**
1.  **Earnings Quality:** The statutory earnings decline is a "red herring." The core business is performing well, but the market may misinterpret the headline numbers. The R445m "ecosystem" spend is a critical swing factor for FY2027 profitability.
2.  **Working Capital Management:** A significant R1.05bn swing in working capital from an outflow to an inflow was the primary driver of the improved operating cash flow. This was achieved through a near-flat inventory level despite 31 new store openings and a R664m increase in trade payables, indicating a deliberate stretch of creditor terms to fund growth.
3.  **Credit Risk:** Trade receivables grew by 15.0%, significantly faster than revenue (9.3%). This, combined with a low ECL allowance, suggests a potential under-provisioning risk, especially if the consumer environment deteriorates further.
8.  **Store Network & Strategy:** The Group is actively evolving its store estate, closing underperforming Baby City stores (reducing from 42 to 0 in the property table, though the narrative says 42 remain) and investing in a new "Store of the Future" format. This transition carries execution risk and potential one-off costs.

### 2. Detailed Forensic Reasoning & Determinations

#### 2.1 Weighted-Average Share Count & HEPS Reconciliation
- **Claim:** Basic HEPS of 113.7 cents and diluted HEPS of 113.6 cents.
9.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED.** The weighted-average number of shares (857,027,527) and diluted shares (857,817,750) are clearly disclosed in Note 11. The HEPS calculation is correctly reconciled from profit attributable to equity holders (R978.9m), adjusting for non-recurring items like the R18.3m JV intangible impairment. The 17.3% decline in HEPS is mathematically correct and is fully explained by the R445m ecosystem investment and non-recurring costs.

#### 2.2 Merchandise vs. Credit/Other Revenue
- **Claim:** Total income includes significant "Other income" of R3.8bn, growing 13.4%.
10.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED with qualifications.** Note 8 decomposes other income into Advertising and marketing income (R1.48bn), Data and administration fees (R2.02bn), Commission (R81m), and Franchise income (R66m). This confirms that a substantial portion of "other income" is supplier rebates and fees, not pure credit or insurance revenue. The auditor's Key Audit Matter (KAM) on rebates confirms the significant judgement involved in classifying these amounts between cost of sales and other income. The growth in other income (13.4%) outpacing revenue (9.3%) is a positive margin driver but also a potential area of accounting discretion.

#### 2.3 Gross Profit Margin Bridge
- **Claim:** Gross profit margin declined from 22.5% to 22.0%.
11.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED.** The arithmetic is correct (Gross Profit R9.41bn / Revenue R42.83bn = 22.0%). The decline is driven by a 10.0% increase in cost of sales, which outpaced the 9.3% revenue growth. This is consistent with the narrative of a constrained consumer and the Group's value-focused "Better Rewards" strategy. The "total income margin" (which includes other income) was better maintained at 30.8% vs 31.0%, highlighting the importance of supplier rebates and other income streams in supporting overall profitability.

#### 2.4 Debtors Book Health & IFRS 9 ECL Provisioning
- **Claim:** Trade receivables grew 15.0% to R3.8bn, with a low ECL allowance.
12.  **Determination:** **HIGH RISK.** Note 29 states that retail receivables from medical aids are "neither past due nor impaired" and that other receivables are from suppliers who are net creditors. This justifies a low ECL provision. However, the 15.0% growth in receivables vs 9.3% revenue growth is a classic red flag. The audit trail does not provide an ageing analysis or the "satisfactory paid" percentage, which are critical for assessing the true health of the debtors book. The risk is that overdue amounts are concealed within "performing" categories. This requires close monitoring.

#### 2.5 Store Network Capex and Trading Density
- **Claim:** 31 new pharmacy stores opened; Baby City network being rationalised.
13.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED with inconsistencies.** The narrative confirms 31 new pharmacy stores and 3 Baby City closures. However, the property table on p. 25 shows a different picture (Dis-Chem stores 289→317, Baby City 41→0), which conflicts with the narrative's 316/42 figures. This suggests the p. 25 table may be a different metric (e.g., gross leasable area) or contains an error. The capex split (R730m expansion vs R374m maintenance) confirms significant investment in new stores and technology. The "Store of the Future" initiative is a key forward-looking strategy to improve trading density and reduce the cost to serve.

#### 2.6 IFRS 16 Lease Liability Structure
- **Claim:** Lease liabilities of R2.94bn.
14.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED.** Note 27.6 confirms new leases of R992m were entered into, and lease repayments were R692m. The interest on lease liabilities was R252m. The maturity profile in Note 29 shows R910m due within 12 months and R2.67bn due thereafter. This is a significant fixed obligation. The group's strategy of purchasing leased properties (like the Columbia Falls acquisition) can reduce future lease liabilities but requires upfront capital.

#### 2.7 Post-Year-End Trading & Outlook
- **Claim:** Strong post-year-end revenue growth of 9.0% and total income margin of 32.0%.
15.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED.** The post-year-end trading update (1 March to 19 May 2026) confirms 9.0% Group revenue growth and a 32.0% total income margin. This is a very positive signal, suggesting the "Better Rewards" program and other initiatives are gaining traction and driving profitability. The guidance for 35 new store openings in FY2027 is also confirmed.

#### 2.8 Cash Conversion & Dividend Payout
- **Claim:** Operating cash flow improved to R1.79bn; dividend payout ratio is ~40%.
16.  **Determination:** **VERIFIED with concerns.** The improvement in operating cash flow was driven by a R1.05bn working capital swing, primarily from a R664m increase in payables. This is not sustainable in the long term. The dividend payout ratio is correctly calculated at ~40% of HEPS. However, the final dividend was cut by 42.8% due to the lower HEPS. The negative free cash flow (operating cash flow less capex and dividends) is a concern and explains the reliance on overdraft facilities.
